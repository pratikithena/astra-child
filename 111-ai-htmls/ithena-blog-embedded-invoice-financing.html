The gap

A hospital pays on its own schedule. The supplier cannot.

The cash-flow gap in healthcare is not a billing failure. It is the arithmetic of two parties on different clocks.

A distributor delivers consumables in March. The health authority processes the invoice on a 30-, 60-, or 90-day term, and often longer if a purchase order line needs matching. Meanwhile the distributor has already paid its own manufacturer, its drivers and its warehouse. Nothing has gone wrong. The money simply is not there yet.

That gap is not rare and it is not shrinking. More than half of European companies — 52% — ran into late-payment problems in 2024. The EU Payment Observatory put the average B2B payment period at 60.3 days in 2025, and Atradius found 47% of B2B invoices in Western Europe were overdue. Healthcare sits at the wrong end of that distribution, because public buyers are slow buyers.

Definition

Embedded means the supplier never leaves your product

Embedded invoice financing

A payment option built into software the supplier already uses, letting them take the money when the invoice is issued instead of when the buyer gets round to paying it.

The distinction that matters is not the financing. Factoring has existed for centuries and European factoring turnover reached €2.055 trillion in 2025 — 11.5% of regional GDP. The distinction is where the decision happens. In the embedded model the supplier is already inside your platform, looking at the invoice they just raised, and there is a button next to it. They press it. The money moves.

Three properties follow from that, and all three are what suppliers actually notice: funds arrive within 24 hours of issue rather than after an approval cycle; the advance is up to 100% of invoice value rather than a partial payment with a retention held back; and there is no application, no open-banking connector to authorise, no document pack to assemble.

The buyer’s side is unchanged. The hospital pays on the date it always intended to pay. The financing provider stands in the middle and carries the wait.

The other side

Care providers keep the terms they negotiated

The objection from the buyer’s finance team is always the same: are we being asked to pay earlier? No.

In a reverse-factoring arrangement the buyer maintains — or extends — its normal payment terms while the supplier is paid immediately. The provider bridges the two dates and carries the exposure between them. Working-capital management on the buyer side is untouched, which is precisely why the buyer will agree to it.

The operational load is also lighter than a treasury team expects: one integration covers 100+ countries and currencies through a single settlement rail, and there is no supplier-onboarding programme to staff. For a healthcare platform selling into several national health systems at once, that is the difference between a product feature and a country-by-country project.

Questions

The six that come up every time

What is healthcare factoring?

A medical supplier or care provider sells its unpaid invoices to a financing provider and receives the cash immediately, instead of waiting the usual 30 to 90 days for the buyer to settle.

Does embedding invoice financing require a lending licence?

No. The financing infrastructure provider holds the licence and supplies the capital, which lets the platform offer financing without becoming a regulated lender itself.

Who takes the loss if a buyer does not pay?

The financing provider. It absorbs credit, fraud, dispute and collections risk. The platform carries zero credit risk.

What does it cost the people using it?

A financing fee on the invoices they choose to fund — nothing on the ones they leave alone. Platforms typically share in that fee, which is what turns the feature into a revenue line rather than a cost centre.

How quickly do suppliers actually get the money?

Within 24 hours of issuing the invoice, for up to 100% of its value. Against 30- to 90-day terms, that is the entire proposition.

Can a small or newly onboarded supplier qualify?

Yes, because the underwriting looks at the buyer rather than the supplier. A supplier with no trading history can finance an invoice owed by a creditworthy hospital.
